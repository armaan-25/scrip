\subsection{Abstract}\label{abstract}

Agent-initiated spending is being deployed faster than any public record
of its failures is accumulating. A survey of the documented record finds
one well-corroborated case of an autonomous agent making an unauthorized
real-money purchase, a small cluster of merchant-side wrong-outcome
failures, and a body of red-team work demonstrating capability without
confirmed victims. That scarcity is a reporting artifact rather than
evidence of reliability: there is no disclosure regime for agent
purchase errors, and individual losses are small enough to be absorbed
by ordinary chargebacks.

Benchmark data fills the gap the incident record leaves. On a
computer-use benchmark, the strongest agent measured succeeds at least
once in ten attempts on roughly 78\% of tasks but succeeds on all ten
attempts for only about 36\%. On a retail benchmark the most
over-represented error category is confirmation handling. A control
designed for an agent that is usually right must therefore assume it
will sometimes be wrong in exactly the step where money moves.

This paper argues that existing agentic payment controls bound the wrong
quantity. Per-transaction amount caps, tokenized credentials, and spend
limits bound \emph{how much} an agent can spend. Every documented
failure surveyed here occurred within an authorized amount, at a
permitted merchant, on a legitimately delegated credential. What was
wrong was the purchase, not the price.

Each leg of the proposed remedy has prior art: deterministic pre-action
authorization, authority bound to a pinned agent configuration, and
outcome-aware settlement have all been proposed separately. The
contribution claimed here is narrower and is the conjunction: one human
approval that binds an exact purchase record by digest \emph{and} a
specific agent version, enforced by pure functions before money moves,
settled against fulfillment evidence the agent did not author, with the
unrecovered residue reported as a first-class figure. A runnable
demonstration against simulated providers shows the property in one
screen: a legitimately authenticated agent, under the amount cap, at a
permitted merchant, proposing the wrong dates, and the payment provider
is never called. A read-only inspection of Natural's API shows where
such a layer would attach and confirms that the rail's event stream
distinguishes money moved from goods delivered only as a post-hoc
chargeback reason code.

\subsection{1. Deployment is outpacing the failure
record}\label{deployment-is-outpacing-the-failure-record}

In February 2025 a technology columnist asked OpenAI's Operator to
compare egg prices in his neighborhood. Within ten minutes the agent
bought a dozen eggs through Instacart and paid for human delivery, a
purchase he had not authorized. The transaction was \$31.43. What makes
it more than an anecdote is that it bypassed OpenAI's own stated
safeguard requiring user confirmation before a purchase; OpenAI
acknowledged the failure and committed to improving those safeguards.
{[}19{]}

That incident is, as far as this survey can establish, the clearest
public case of an autonomous agent making an unauthorized real-money
purchase. It is nearly the only one.

This is not a claim that agent purchase errors are rare. It is a claim
about the record. Searching the incident databases, technology press,
security research, and regulatory publications for documented
agent-initiated purchase failures returns a very short list, assembled
in Section 2. Meanwhile the infrastructure for such purchases has been
deployed broadly: card networks have published agent transaction
protocols {[}6{]} {[}5{]}, a major marketplace operates an agentic
checkout against third-party catalogs {[}21{]}, a card issuer has
committed to indemnify registered-agent purchase errors {[}35{]}, and
consumer assistants that transact on a user's behalf have raised
substantial capital and reached general availability.

Several structural reasons explain the gap, none of them reassuring:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{No disclosure regime.} No rule requires a platform to publish
  an agent purchase error. Voluntary transparency reporting so far has
  covered model behavior rather than transactions; a September 2026
  disclosure of six agent incidents by OpenAI contained no purchase,
  payment, or booking events, all six having occurred in internal
  testing. {[}25{]}
\item
  \textbf{Losses are small enough to disappear.} A \$31 error resolves
  through an ordinary chargeback or a goodwill refund and never becomes
  a public record.
\item
  \textbf{Merchant-side harms settle privately.} Where the injured party
  is a business rather than a consumer, the dispute resolves
  contractually. This survey found no documented enterprise procurement
  agent failure, which is more plausibly a disclosure artifact than an
  absence of error.
\end{itemize}

The honest formulation is that agent-initiated spending is being
deployed considerably faster than public evidence about its reliability
is accumulating. A design discipline for this domain therefore cannot
wait for the incident record to mature, and should not be calibrated to
the small number of failures that happen to have been reported.

\subsection{2. What the record does
contain}\label{what-the-record-does-contain}

Sources were accessed September 18 and 19, 2026. Each item below is
labeled by evidence tier. Two claims encountered during this survey, a
statistic on agent dispute rates and a purported regulatory advisory on
autonomous-agent purchases, could not be substantiated at their
attributed sources and are deliberately excluded.

\subsubsection{2.1 Liability is already settled, and it sits with the
operator}\label{liability-is-already-settled-and-it-sits-with-the-operator}

Before the agentic era, a Canadian tribunal ruled on an airline chatbot
that gave a passenger incorrect information about a bereavement fare,
causing him to pay \$1,630.36 in full fare. The airline argued that the
chatbot was ``a separate legal entity'' for which it was not
responsible. The tribunal rejected this, holding that the company ``is
responsible for all the information on its website'' and that it ``makes
no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a
chatbot,'' and awarded damages. {[}20{]}

\emph{Evidence tier: court ruling.} This is the strongest item in the
survey and the most consequential. It establishes that an operator owns
its agent's errors. Any architecture that leaves an agent's wrong
purchase unattributable is therefore not merely a technical gap; it is
an unmanaged liability.

\subsubsection{2.2 Authorized purchase, wrong
outcome}\label{authorized-purchase-wrong-outcome}

This is the failure mode central to this paper, and the most thinly
documented.

In December 2024 a reporter asked a shopping agent to buy a specific
toothpaste from a named retailer. The agent took payment, confirmed on
the reporter's bank statement, then failed for three hours without
completing the purchase. The item was in fact out of stock at the
retailer while appearing available in the agent's scraped search data.
{[}22{]} \emph{Evidence tier: named-source journalism, firsthand, single
source.} Authorization was valid at every layer. The agent's model of
the world was wrong.

At larger scale, a marketplace agentic checkout placed orders against
stale, incorrect, or entirely absent third-party catalog data beginning
around November 2025. Named merchants reported receiving orders for
products they do not sell, listings displaying photographs of unrelated
items, and orders for discontinued stock. When the marketplace's data
was wrong, the merchant absorbed return shipping and refund. One
affected merchant collected 145 responses from other brands. {[}21{]}
\emph{Evidence tier: named-source journalism, multiple named merchants,
two outlets.}

The marketplace case inverts the usual framing: the consumer's
authorization was entirely valid and the injured party was the merchant.
It is the only fleet-scale evidence located in this survey.

\subsubsection{2.3 Irreversible action against an explicit
constraint}\label{irreversible-action-against-an-explicit-constraint}

In July 2025 a coding agent, operating under an explicit instruction not
to modify production during a code freeze, executed destructive commands
against a live production database, deleting records for approximately
1,200 companies and a comparable number of individuals. It then reported
fabricated results and incorrectly asserted that recovery was
impossible. The data was recovered manually; the vendor apologized and
shipped environment separation and a non-executing planning mode within
days. {[}23{]} \emph{Evidence tier: firsthand, company acknowledgment,
journalism.}

This was not a financial transaction and is included only for the
mechanism it demonstrates: an agent taking an irreversible action that
the operator had explicitly forbidden, then misreporting it. The same
mechanism applied to a payment produces a loss that no amount limit
would have prevented.

\subsubsection{2.4 Injection-driven spending: capability, not
casualties}\label{injection-driven-spending-capability-not-casualties}

Security researchers have catalogued prompt-injection payloads on live
web pages that specifically target payment actions, including attempts
to subscribe a victim to a paid plan, force a donation through a payment
link, and redirect a substantial transfer to an attacker-controlled
account. The publishing researchers state explicitly that they are not
aware of any confirmed real-world instance where such an attack
succeeded against a deployed system. {[}24{]} Separate vulnerability
research has demonstrated indirect injection against agentic browsers,
including payloads concealed in images. {[}26{]}

\emph{Evidence tier: vendor security research.} The accurate formulation
is that the capability is demonstrated and payloads are present in the
wild, while no confirmed consumer losses have been published. This must
not be presented as an incident record. It does establish that an
agent's instruction stream is attacker-reachable, which bears directly
on why authority should bind to terms a human approved rather than to
the agent's in-flight reasoning.

\subsection{3. The rate, which the anecdotes cannot
supply}\label{the-rate-which-the-anecdotes-cannot-supply}

Because the incident record is thin, the more informative evidence is
measured error rates, and specifically the gap between an agent
succeeding \emph{once} and succeeding \emph{every time}.

The cleanest measurement of that gap is a 2026 study of computer-use
agents on the OSWorld benchmark. {[}29{]} Its headline finding: for the
strongest configuration measured (Agent S3 with GPT-5), Pass@10, the
fraction of tasks the agent completes at least once in ten independent
attempts, reaches approximately 78\%. Pass\^{}10, the fraction of tasks
it completes on \emph{all} ten attempts, is approximately 36\%. Same
agent, same tasks, same environment. The 42-point spread between those
numbers is the quantity a payment control has to be designed around. The
authors attribute the spread to three sources, execution stochasticity,
task-specification ambiguity, and variability in agent behavior, and the
second of those is what Section 7.3 of this paper addresses directly.

An earlier retail-and-airline benchmark reported the same shape under
the name pass\^{}k, and remains the source for the domain-specific error
taxonomy. {[}18{]} On its retail domain, a model measured at roughly
61\% correct on a single attempt fell to roughly 25\% correct across
eight consecutive attempts. Frontier models have since improved
single-attempt retail performance substantially; the shape of the
decline is the durable finding. Two further details from that work bear
on this paper directly. First, the benchmark's retail error taxonomy
over-represents \emph{confirmation handling} errors relative to other
domains, the agent mishandling the step where it should have checked
with the user. That is precisely the failure exhibited in the Operator
incident, where a stated confirmation safeguard did not fire. Second, it
also over-represents \emph{constraint interpretation} errors, the agent
misreading what it was told to satisfy. Both benchmarks are preprints;
neither is cited here as peer-reviewed.

One further result bears on version binding specifically. A study of LLM
shopping agents across model versions found that ``model updates can
drastically reshuffle market shares'': the same agent, upgraded, buys
different things. {[}34{]} An authorization that survives a model update
is therefore an authorization for behavior nobody approved.

A spending agent operates in the repeated regime, not the single-attempt
one. A control designed on the assumption that the agent is usually
correct is calibrated to the wrong statistic.

\subsection{4. Why current controls do not address these
failures}\label{why-current-controls-do-not-address-these-failures}

The controls shipped with current agentic payment products are, with one
exception, quantity controls.

A consumer assistant that transacts on a user's behalf mints a
single-use virtual card authorized for a specific amount, so that the
agent never handles the underlying card number. {[}9{]} This is a
genuine and well-designed control: it bounds the exposure of any one
transaction and removes the credential from the agent's reach entirely.

A payments platform for agents enforces permissions and limits
server-side on every request, so that a compromised or misbehaving agent
cannot exceed what it was granted. Limits may be set per transaction,
per day, and per month. A payment breaching a limit is not rejected; it
is held and an approval is opened that the agent cannot clear itself.
{[}7{]} Server-side evaluation and the inability of an agent to raise
its own caps are both correct design choices, and stronger than
client-side enforcement. Section 8.1 reports what the same platform's
API actually exposes.

Neither control addresses any failure in Section 2.

The unauthorized egg purchase was for \$31.43, within any plausible
limit. The toothpaste purchase was the right item at the right merchant
for the right price; only the inventory was wrong. The marketplace
orders were correctly priced against catalogs that were themselves
incorrect. In each case an amount-based control evaluates the
transaction and finds nothing to object to, because there was nothing
wrong with the amount.

Two further properties of the current model deserve note:

\textbf{Holding rather than blocking relocates the decision but does not
make it deterministic.} A limit breach that opens an approval is a good
design for the case where the user is available and understands what
they are approving. It does not help where the purchase is within
limits, which is the case in every incident surveyed.

\textbf{Where confirmation is discretionary, it is not a control.} One
consumer assistant's terms appoint the service as the user's agent to
``enter into agreements, commitments or transactions'' on their behalf,
then state that the provider ``may implement safeguards, confirmation
requirements, or other controls on certain Actions'' while making ``no
representation or warranty that such safeguards will prevent unintended
or erroneous Actions.'' The same terms make payments non-refundable,
direct purchase disputes to the merchant rather than the provider, and
cap provider liability at one hundred dollars. {[}27{]} An independent
assessment of the same product found that confirmation before charge
does sometimes occur. {[}28{]} A safeguard that fires sometimes, is
disclaimed contractually, and is paired with no recovery path is not a
control a user can reason about.

\subsubsection{4.1 The indemnification
counter-argument}\label{the-indemnification-counter-argument}

The strongest objection to this paper is that the problem has already
been priced rather than prevented. In April 2026 American Express
announced an agentic commerce developer kit and, alongside it, Amex
Agent Purchase Protection: ``if a Card Member authorizes an AI agent to
make a purchase and that agent sends American Express the customer's
authenticated purchase intent, American Express will protect eligible
customers from charges related to AI agent error.'' {[}35{]} If the
issuer absorbs the loss, why build a prevention layer at all?

Three answers, in increasing order of weight.

First, the commitment is conditional and forward-looking. The release's
own language is ``in the future,'' the protection applies to ``eligible
customers,'' and it is filed under the release's
forward-looking-statements caution. It is a stated direction, not a live
guarantee, and this paper cites it as such.

Second, the condition is the point. Protection attaches only when the
agent ``sends American Express the customer's authenticated purchase
intent.'' The kit's components are named accordingly: Agent
Registration, Intent Intelligence ``to ensure Card Member purchase
intent is accurately captured to support authentication, authorization
and disputes,'' and Cart Context ``to enhance validation, authorizations
and dispute investigations.'' In other words, the indemnity is
underwritten by exactly the artifact this paper proposes to produce: an
exact, authenticated record of what the human approved, captured before
the transaction. An issuer cannot distinguish ``agent error'' from
``customer changed their mind'' without it. The prevention layer and the
indemnity are not alternatives; the indemnity presupposes the prevention
layer's output.

Third, indemnification covers the cardholder and no one else. The
marketplace case in Section 2.2 injured merchants, who absorbed refunds
and return shipping against orders no cardholder disputed. The
toothpaste case produced a three-hour failure and a bank-statement
charge that an indemnity would eventually reverse but a preflight check
would never have created. And a closed-loop issuer's protection ends at
that issuer's network; a wallet-based rail such as the one examined in
Section 8.1 has no equivalent commitment. An indemnity is a good
backstop. It is a poor primary control, because it is triggered by
exactly the loss a deterministic check would have prevented, and it is
available only to some of the parties who bear that loss.

The missing axis is not \emph{how much} but \emph{what}, and
\emph{whether it worked}.

\subsection{5. Related work}\label{related-work}

This section positions the proposal against the literature. The area is
crowded, and the honest statement is that no single leg of the proposed
structure is new. What this review did not find is the conjunction, and
Section 5.4 states the gaps that survived it.

Sources were accessed September 17 through 19, 2026. Of the roughly
forty papers examined, three are peer-reviewed: an agent-security
benchmark {[}37{]} and two law-review treatments of agent liability
{[}38{]} {[}39{]}. Everything else, including the four nearest
neighbours below, is a preprint and is labeled as such.

\subsubsection{5.1 Delegated spending authority for
agents}\label{delegated-spending-authority-for-agents}

This is the crowded area, roughly a dozen papers. Four are close enough
that a reader should check them before crediting this paper with
anything.

\textbf{Formal analysis of agent payment protocols.} A Tamarin-based
analysis of x402, MPP, ACP, and AP2 across 86 verification cases finds
40 previously undocumented issues, and concludes that ``delegated
authorization must remain consistent with its resulting economic and
service effects across actors, states, and protocol stages.'' {[}30{]}
That sentence is close to this paper's thesis, arrived at by formal
methods rather than incident survey. The difference is scope: that work
verifies the protocols as specified; this paper argues about what the
specifications omit, namely an exact purchase record and a fulfillment
fact.

\textbf{Deterministic pre-action authorization.} A March 2026 preprint
proposes intercepting tool calls ``synchronously before execution,''
evaluating them ``against a declarative policy,'' and producing ``a
cryptographically signed audit record,'' reporting zero successful
adversarial actions under its policy against 74.6\% under a permissive
one. {[}31{]} It uses the same words this paper uses. It is a general
tool-call gate; it does not bind to a human-approved purchase digest, a
pinned agent version, or a fulfillment outcome.

\textbf{Authority under agent evolution.} A July 2026 preprint asks the
version-binding question directly: whether a grant survives a mutation
of the agent, proposing ``an immutable effect ceiling established at
grant time'' below which ``authority may contract freely and expand only
under specified evidence conditions.'' {[}32{]} This paper's
authorized-version allow-list and change classification (Section 7.4)
are a concrete instance of that framing for one domain. That work owns
the question; this paper implements one answer.

\textbf{Mandate signatures are not intent.} A systematic security
analysis of AP2 v0.2 identifies 48 threats and demonstrates that ``valid
mandate signatures alone do not ensure that an agent-mediated
transaction reflects the user's intent when its pre-authorization
context is manipulated.'' {[}33{]} This is the strongest argument in the
literature for evaluating the approved record with a pure function
rather than trusting the signature on it, and Section 7.2 relies on it.

Beyond those four: OAuth Rich Authorization Requests already expresses
payment amount and recipient in structured form {[}2{]}; AP2 defines
Checkout and Payment Mandates with linked receipts {[}3{]} {[}4{]};
Mastercard's Verifiable Intent (draft v0.1) specifies a delegation chain
and machine-enforceable purchase constraints while leaving dispute
resolution out of scope {[}5{]}; Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol specifies
signed agent-recognition messages {[}6{]}. None of these evaluates
fulfillment.

\subsubsection{5.2 Prompt injection and agent
security}\label{prompt-injection-and-agent-security}

AgentDojo (NeurIPS 2024) is the peer-reviewed benchmark for injection
attacks on tool-using agents. {[}37{]} CaMeL, a preprint, proposes
separating control flow from untrusted data so that injected content
cannot reach a privileged action. {[}40{]} Both motivate the design rule
in Section 7.2 that a model's in-flight assessment is never an
enforcement mechanism. Neither addresses payments specifically.

\subsubsection{5.3 Identity, provenance, and outcome
verification}\label{identity-provenance-and-outcome-verification}

SPIFFE/SPIRE separates workload identity from credentials {[}10{]}; SLSA
describes build provenance {[}11{]}; the IETF RATS architecture
distinguishes evidence, appraisal, and attestation results with explicit
freshness assumptions {[}12{]}. These are the foundations a
stronger-than-self-declared attestation level would reuse (Section 9.3),
not infrastructure this paper replaces.

On outcome verification: ERC-8004 proposes identity, reputation, and
validation registries with payments orthogonal {[}13{]}. The Agentic
Settlement Protocol (September 2026 preprint) proposes holds,
fulfillment verification, and partial refunds for delayed-fulfillment
stablecoin commerce, including travel, and identifies itself as a design
paper without deployed measurements. {[}14{]} Classical escrow work for
digital goods explicitly assumes the good is hash-verifiable. {[}36{]}
Outcome-aware settlement is therefore not novel; what remains open is
stated next.

\subsubsection{5.4 What survived the
review}\label{what-survived-the-review}

Four gaps were checked for and not found occupied.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Outcome verification for physical goods and services.} The
  escrow and attestation literature verifies digital delivery by hash. A
  hotel night, a delivered grocery order, or a booked flight has no
  hash. Section 7.5's treatment of independently sourced fulfillment
  evidence with an explicit \texttt{unknown} outcome is offered against
  this gap.
\item
  \textbf{Authorized-but-not-wanted as a first-class category.} Industry
  names it (``agent chargebacks,'' ``AI agent error''); the academic
  literature treats it as either fraud or as a UX problem. It is
  neither: the authorization was valid and the purchase was wrong.
\item
  \textbf{UETA §10 applied to LLM agents.} The Uniform Electronic
  Transactions Act lets an individual avoid the effect of an automated
  transaction where the electronic agent's provider gave no ``means to
  prevent or correct the error.'' {[}41{]} No peer-reviewed treatment
  applies this to LLM-driven purchasing. If it applies, a provider that
  offers no preflight is not merely exposed to loss; it may be exposed
  to avoidance of the transaction itself. This paper flags the hook and
  does not resolve it.
\item
  \textbf{Real-money failure rates.} Every benchmark in Section 3 is
  sandboxed. Nobody has published what fraction of live agent purchases
  are wrong.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{5.5 Contribution}\label{contribution}

Against that map, the claim is the conjunction: (a) deterministic
pre-action authorization evaluated by pure functions, (b) over an exact
human-approved record identified by digest, (c) bound to an explicit
allow-list of agent versions, (d) settled against fulfillment evidence
with source attribution and a three-valued outcome, (e) with recovery
modeled as a workflow that reports its residue. Each of (a) through (e)
has a nearest neighbour above. The joint is unoccupied, and the
demonstration in Section 9 is offered as evidence that the joint is
constructible, not that it is complete.

\subsection{6. System model and trust
assumptions}\label{system-model-and-trust-assumptions}

The \textbf{principal} grants authority. An \textbf{operator} deploys
the agent. A \textbf{control service} authenticates requests, checks
mandates, and maintains budget and operation state. \textbf{Payment
providers} report financial events. \textbf{Evidence providers} report
fulfillment or recovery events. Some roles may belong to one
organization, but their claims should remain distinguishable.

The agent may generate mistaken requests, encounter malicious content,
repeat operations, or continue after an operator changes its
configuration. Providers may time out, return events out of order, or
supply inconsistent evidence. These are the failures the design aims to
expose or constrain.

The intended controls require all covered spending to pass through the
service or equivalent provider-enforced restrictions. An agent with an
unrestricted credential outside that boundary can bypass them. The
design assumes a trustworthy authorization service, protected state,
authenticated principal actions, and appropriate verification of
provider messages. A compromised administrator, colluding evidence
source, or stolen credential can invalidate those assumptions. Storing a
hash beside data does not protect both from an attacker who can rewrite
the database.

``Verified outcome'' means an assessment supported by specified evidence
under a stated trust policy. It does not mean objective truth, customer
satisfaction, legal liability, or proof of the agent's private
reasoning.

Manifests and receipts can reveal sensitive instructions, destinations,
purchases, and behavior. The proposed service should minimize
disclosure, restrict access, and define retention periods. A digest is
not encryption: predictable underlying content may still be guessed.
Recording private chain-of-thought is neither necessary nor an assurance
mechanism in this design.

\subsection{7. A deterministic authorization
structure}\label{a-deterministic-authorization-structure}

The goal is not to make agent behavior deterministic; it is not. The
goal is to make the \emph{authorization decision} deterministic, so that
whatever the agent does, the set of purchases it can cause is a pure
function of a record a human approved.

The structure has three phases, and the boundary between the first and
the third is the whole idea:

\begin{verbatim}
PROPOSE   nondeterministic. A model drafts the purchase and extracts typed
          constraints. Anything it cannot type is recorded as unresolved,
          and unresolved blocks.
RATIFY    a human approves once. Approval binds sha256(canonical(record))
          and an explicit agent version.
ENFORCE   deterministic. Pure functions only; no model runs. preflight()
          gates the payment call; assess() reads payment and fulfillment
          evidence and returns success, failure, or unknown.
\end{verbatim}

This section states the structure independently of any implementation.
Section 9 describes a prototype and a demonstration that realize it for
one purchase category.

\subsubsection{7.1 Approve an exact record, not a
budget}\label{approve-an-exact-record-not-a-budget}

A spending authorization should name the purchase, not a budget. ``A
refundable room at this hotel, these dates, this rate'' rather than
``hotels under \$550.'' The approved record is canonicalized and hashed;
the hash is what the human's approval binds to.

This inverts the usual tradeoff. A budget is convenient and admits every
failure in Section 2, because every one of those purchases was inside
its budget. An exact record is less convenient and admits none of them:
a cancellation is not a purchase in the record, a different item is not
the item in the record, an out-of-stock substitution is not the approved
quote.

The cost is real and should be stated. Exact approval means an agent
that finds a better option must return for a new approval, and a system
that requires many approvals may be abandoned or approved reflexively.
Section 10 treats this as the central open question rather than a solved
problem.

\subsubsection{7.2 Constraints must be
predicates}\label{constraints-must-be-predicates}

Conditions that govern payment must have machine-checkable
representations, amount ceilings, permitted merchants, date ranges,
refundability deadlines, evaluated by a pure function over the approved
record and the proposed purchase. Natural language may propose these
terms; it must not enforce them. A model's assertion that a purchase
looks acceptable is not an enforcement mechanism, and per {[}33{]},
neither is a valid signature over a mandate whose context was
manipulated before signing.

\subsubsection{7.3 What cannot be typed must
block}\label{what-cannot-be-typed-must-block}

A condition the system cannot represent as a predicate (``somewhere
nice'', ``good reviews'') is the dangerous case, because the natural
implementation is to drop it. The structure requires that unresolved
conditions be recorded as such and that their presence block unattended
execution. Failing closed on uninterpretable terms is what prevents a
silent gap between what the human said and what the system enforces.

This also localizes the nondeterminism that natural-language
interpretation necessarily introduces. Extraction from prose is
fallible; the design requirement is not that extraction be correct but
that it be \emph{calibrated}: confidently mistyping a constraint is the
harmful failure, while declining to type one is safe because it blocks.
A useful evaluation of such a system measures silent mistyping against
correct refusal, not extraction accuracy. This is also the paper's
answer to the task-specification ambiguity that {[}29{]} identifies as a
source of unreliability: ambiguity is surfaced at RATIFY, not resolved
silently at ENFORCE.

\subsubsection{7.4 Authority binds to a declared
configuration}\label{authority-binds-to-a-declared-configuration}

An authorization granted to an agent is granted to a particular declared
configuration of that agent: model reference, instruction and policy
digests, code artifact, tool versions and permissions. A newly
registered configuration inherits no authority; a change to any
authority-bearing field requires review under the principal's policy,
and a change whose behavioral effect is unknown, a dependency bump, a
code change labeled a patch, requires review rather than receiving an
automatic exemption.

Authority is checked before any exposure is reserved, and rechecked
immediately before the external call. The second check exists because
revocation can arrive while an operation is in flight. It narrows the
window; it does not close it. Ordering between a local registry and an
external provider is not atomic, and once a provider has accepted an
instruction, revocation cannot retract it. That operation must be
reconciled under the configuration that issued it rather than disowned.

\subsubsection{7.5 Outcome is assessed from evidence the agent did not
write}\label{outcome-is-assessed-from-evidence-the-agent-did-not-write}

Payment success and fulfillment success are separate facts and must be
recorded separately. Capture is recorded when financial evidence
supports it, even where fulfillment later fails; delaying recognition of
spent money until the outcome is satisfactory misstates exposure.

Three evidence streams are kept apart by source:
\texttt{payment\_observed}, authoritative for money;
\texttt{fulfillment\_observed}, authoritative for delivery; and
\texttt{execution\_observed}, the agent's own narrative, which is logged
and never read by the assessor. The assessor returns three outcomes, not
two. Where payment and fulfillment facts conflict, or two fulfillment
sources disagree, it returns \texttt{unknown} and stops authority for
human review rather than guessing. This is the property that would have
caught the toothpaste case: payment captured, fulfillment absent,
assessment not successful.

A caveat this structure cannot escape: source attribution on evidence is
only as good as the adapter that produced it. Labeling a record as
merchant-originated does not authenticate the merchant. An
adapter-signed HMAC over (operation key, external id, payload)
authenticates the \emph{adapter}, not the merchant. Evidence
authenticity is a deployment obligation, and a system that treats a
source label as proof has moved the trust problem rather than solved it.

\subsubsection{7.6 Recovery is a workflow, not an
undo}\label{recovery-is-a-workflow-not-an-undo}

Prevention is cheap and reliable: a pure function evaluated before money
moves. Recovery is expensive and unreliable: it depends on merchants,
networks, and deadlines outside the system's control. The design
consequence is that effort belongs in prevention, and that recovery must
never be described as an undo.

Concretely, the structure requires that a refund \emph{request} and a
\emph{posted} refund be distinct recorded facts; that a posted refund
restore spending authority only where an approved policy says it does,
so that repeated purchase-and-refund cycles cannot evade a cumulative
limit; that disputes require explicit human authorization rather than
being filed automatically; and that a receipt report an
\emph{unrecovered} amount as a first-class figure. A system claiming
complete recovery would be misrepresenting what it controls. Reporting
the residue is the honest alternative.

\subsection{8. Fitting existing
infrastructure}\label{fitting-existing-infrastructure}

This structure is an authorization and accountability layer. It is not a
payment rail, and implementing it does not require custody, card
issuance, a new token, a blockchain, or a legal identity standard. It
sits above a rail and calls it.

That placement is deliberate, and it is what makes the structure
complementary to current agentic payment platforms rather than
competitive with them. A platform that holds funds, enforces permissions
server-side, and moves money on instruction supplies exactly the
execution layer this structure assumes. What the structure adds is
upstream of the payment call and downstream of it: an exact approved
record that determines whether the call should be made at all, and an
outcome assessment that determines what happens afterward.

\subsubsection{8.1 What Natural's API exposes: a read-only
inspection}\label{what-naturals-api-exposes-a-read-only-inspection}

On September 19, 2026, Natural's API was inspected through its MCP
server under an agent-scoped OAuth grant (actor type \texttt{AGENT},
credential kind \texttt{oauth\_grant}), plus its published API
reference. {[}42{]} {[}43{]} {[}44{]} Calls made: identity, party
limits, agent list, transaction list, customer list, external-account
list. No payment, transfer, request, deposit, or withdrawal was created;
no money moved. The account held no transactions, so event payloads
below are quoted from the published event catalog rather than observed
live. Three questions were put to the product.

\textbf{Can a payment carry an opaque digest of an approved purchase?}
Yes, as a passenger. A payment accepts \texttt{tags}, at most 30 keys,
keys 1 to 128 alphanumeric-or-underscore characters, values 1 to 256
characters; a \texttt{description} of at most 80 characters; an
\texttt{Idempotency-Key} header of at most 255 characters; and an
\texttt{X-Instance-ID} header of at most 1024 characters that the docs
describe as a ``caller-chosen identifier for the agent run, session, or
conversation, required when an agent moves money.'' A 64-character hex
SHA-256 fits in any of these. \texttt{tags} is the correct carrier: it
is returned on the Payment object and inside the
\texttt{payment.completed} webhook payload, so a control layer's
contract digest would ride out on the call and come back on every event
about it. Natural does not interpret tags. This makes the digest a
correlation handle for the RATIFY-to-ENFORCE link, not an enforcement
point. Enforcement would remain upstream, in the layer that decides
whether to call \texttt{create\_payment} at all, which is where Section
7 puts it.

\textbf{What does a mandate contain beyond amount limits?} The answer
depends on which of two objects ``mandate'' names, and neither carries a
purchase.

The agent-side grant is the Agent object: \texttt{name},
\texttt{description}, \texttt{tags}, \texttt{handle}, \texttt{status}
(ACTIVE or REVOKED), \texttt{createdBy}, \texttt{lastActiveAt}, and
\texttt{limits} with three fields, \texttt{perTransaction},
\texttt{perDay}, and \texttt{perMonth}, each an integer in cents or
null. Party-level limits are the same three. The permission set on the
inspected grant was a flat list of eleven scopes
(\texttt{payments.create}, \texttt{payments.read},
\texttt{wallets.transfer}, and so on). There is no merchant field, no
category, no item, no date, no recipient allow-list, and no digest. This
is the model Section 4 describes: correct, server-enforced, and entirely
about quantity.

Natural also has a resource literally named \texttt{mandate}, with
\texttt{mandate.created}, \texttt{mandate.updated}, and
\texttt{mandate.revoked} events. Its published payload is a bank-debit
authorization: \texttt{scheme:\ "ach\_web"}, \texttt{amountType},
\texttt{amount} in minor units, \texttt{frequency:\ "recurring"},
\texttt{merchantId}, \texttt{effectiveAt}, \texttt{expiresAt},
supersession links, and an \texttt{evidence} object containing
\texttt{captureMethod}, \texttt{capturedAt}, \texttt{ipAddress},
\texttt{authorizationText}, and \texttt{authorizationUri}. That evidence
object is worth noting precisely because of what it is: evidence that
\emph{consent was captured}, of the kind NACHA rules require for a WEB
debit. It is not evidence about any outcome. So the one place Natural's
API records an authorization artifact with provenance, it records the
provenance of the permission, not of the purchase. A memo to Natural
should use the word ``mandate'' carefully; in their vocabulary it
already means something.

\textbf{Does anything in the event stream distinguish money moved from
goods delivered?} On the payment leg, no. The Payment status enum is
CREATED, PROCESSING, PENDING\_CLAIM, IN\_REVIEW, COMPLETED, FAILED,
RETURNED, APPROVAL\_DENIED, CANCELED. Every value describes the money.
The event catalog has no fulfillment, delivery, receipt, or confirmation
event for any resource. The term ``fulfill'' does appear, in
\texttt{fulfill\_payment\_request}, and it means the \emph{payer paying}
a request, a term collision the memo should flag before it causes
confusion.

The one place delivery enters the product is the card-acquiring stream:
\texttt{chargeback.created} carries a \texttt{reason} object, and the
published example is
\texttt{\{\ category:\ "notReceived",\ networkCode:\ "13.1",\ description:\ "Merchandise\ or\ services\ not\ received"\ \}}.
That is the network's own vocabulary for the failure this paper is
about. It appears only as a dispute reason, raised by a cardholder after
the fact, on card payments, and it opens a case that ``starts with
response, representment, and closure stages.'' In other words: the
distinction exists in the product exactly once, as a post-hoc chargeback
code, and never as a signal on the leg where the agent paid. The claim
in the abstract is therefore confirmed with one refinement, and the
refinement strengthens it. Natural already has a place to put a
fulfillment fact, the chargeback reason taxonomy, and no way to learn
that fact before a customer complains.

Two further observations from the inspection. The MCP server refuses
\texttt{list\_wallets} for agent-attributed calls because the list ``is
party-wide and can expose ungranted wallets or vault balances''; this is
a least-exposure decision made in the right direction and worth
acknowledging. And Natural's own founder essay states that ``the market
for agent to agent payments has also not yet materialized'' and that
``you own the market logic (targeting, sourcing, pricing, and
fulfillment), and Natural moves each customer's money inside the limits
they delegated.'' {[}1{]} That is a precise statement of the boundary.
Everything in Section 7 lives on the customer's side of it.

\subsubsection{8.2 Where the layer would
attach}\label{where-the-layer-would-attach}

Mapped onto Natural's surface, the three phases land as follows. PROPOSE
and RATIFY are entirely outside Natural; they produce a digest.
ENFORCE's \texttt{preflight()} runs before \texttt{create\_payment}; on
pass, the call carries the digest in \texttt{tags} and the mission's
operation key as \texttt{Idempotency-Key}, and the mission id as
\texttt{X-Instance-ID}. \texttt{payment.completed} (or \texttt{failed},
\texttt{returned}) becomes the \texttt{payment\_observed} stream,
matched back by tag. \texttt{fulfillment\_observed} comes from a
merchant or email adapter Natural does not provide. \texttt{assess()}
runs on the pair. If a \texttt{chargeback.created} later arrives with
category \texttt{notReceived}, that is a fourth evidence stream and a
useful one: it is the network telling the control layer its assessment
was wrong.

Two constraints on this sketch. Natural's acceptable-use policy
prohibits building a competing product with its technology; evaluating
the product and writing about it is what this section does, and wiring
Natural in as a rail for a competing wallet is not proposed. And the
boundary caveat from Section 6 applies with full force: this works only
when the agent's sole funded credential is one the control layer holds.
An agent with its own Natural agent key routes around the layer
entirely, which is the same caveat Natural's documentation makes about
its own limits.

Mapping onto other published work: the card networks' agent transaction
protocols are converging on cryptographically binding a transaction to a
human-approved mandate specifying merchant categories, caps, and time
windows {[}6{]} {[}5{]}; the structure here carries the same idea from
categories and caps to the exact purchase, and from authorization to
outcome. Rich authorization requests {[}2{]} and intent protocols
{[}3{]} provide transport for structured authorization detail; an
integration would require an agreed constraint schema and a verification
algorithm, not simply an additional field. Workload identity and
attestation work {[}10{]} {[}11{]} {[}12{]} provides the machinery that
would raise version binding above the declared level. Payment adapters
must carry provider-specific rules for idempotency retention,
authorization expiry, partial capture, refund states, and disputes
{[}16{]} {[}17{]}; any field that cannot be faithfully enforced on a
given rail should produce a restricted mode or a refusal rather than a
claim of equivalent protection.

\subsection{9. What the prototype and the demonstration
establish}\label{what-the-prototype-and-the-demonstration-establish}

A local TypeScript prototype (Scrip) implements the structure for one
purchase category, an exact, human-approved hotel booking, against
simulated payment and execution providers. It has never moved real
money, issued a real card, or filed a dispute. The observations below
come from source inspection, local tests, and one scripted run.

Implemented: an approved contract canonicalized and hashed, with
approval binding to that hash; typed constraint predicates evaluated by
a pure function before any reservation; unresolved constraints blocking
approval; agent lineage, immutable version records with manifest
digests, and mandates carrying an explicit authorized-version
allow-list; authority checked before reservation and again before
dispatch; exposure reserved durably before any provider call; outcome
assessed from merchant and payment facts with the agent's narrative
excluded; refund request and posted refund recorded separately; and a
receipt reporting authorized, reserved, captured, reversed, refunded,
returned, and unrecovered amounts as distinct figures.

\subsubsection{9.1 The demonstration}\label{the-demonstration}

\texttt{npm\ run\ demo:deterministic} runs the three phases end to end
in under two seconds against fake providers, with no network access and
temporary databases deleted on exit. The screen that carries the
argument is the first enforcement step:

\begin{verbatim}
3a. Agent proposes: same hotel, same room, same price, Sept 19-21.
X  BLOCKED before payment
    Constraint mismatch: date_range
    Exact purchase changed; revise and approve a new version

    amount $500 <= $550 cap        passes    ← an amount limit stops here
    merchant hotel.example        permitted
    agent credential              authentic, version authorized
    date_range 09-18..09-20       mismatch  ← preflight() stops here

    payments.issue() calls: 0    execution.start() calls: 0
\end{verbatim}

The candidate is under the cap, at the permitted merchant, presented by
an authenticated agent whose version the mandate authorizes. Every
quantity control passes it. The date is wrong by one day,
\texttt{preflight()} refuses it, and the payment provider is never
called. There is nothing to refund because nothing was reserved.

The run also shows: the PROPOSE phase failing closed on an untypeable
condition (``somewhere quiet'') so that ratification is refused until
the human resolves it; a forged credential rejected at the service
boundary; the exact purchase paid, confirmed by the merchant stream, and
assessed \texttt{success} from two independent sources while the agent's
narrative is logged and ignored; and a second mission in which payment
captures but the merchant reports the booking failed, assessed
\texttt{failure} with \texttt{unrecovered\ \$500.00} on the receipt
until a refund actually posts, at which point \texttt{unrecovered}
returns to zero. The last scenario is the toothpaste case, and it is the
one a rail whose lifecycle ends at COMPLETED reports as a success. A
regression test (\texttt{tests/demo-deterministic.test.ts}) asserts each
of those outcomes, including that the provider call count after the
wrong-date candidate is zero.

What the demonstration does not show: the PROPOSE phase is scripted, not
model-driven, so extraction calibration (Section 7.3) is asserted rather
than measured; both providers are in-process fakes; the merchant
evidence is labeled, not authenticated.

\subsubsection{9.3 Limits}\label{limits}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{No measured-runtime guarantee.} Registration is self-declared
  and that is the only level the registry can issue; the interface for
  stronger attestation exists with no implementation, so a stronger
  label cannot be produced. A manifest digest detects alteration of the
  record, not of the running program. An unchanged digest cannot detect
  undeclared changes, mutable model aliases, or evolving retrieved
  content.
\item
  \textbf{Human authentication is out of scope.} The principal is a
  caller-supplied identifier. Approval and revocation assume an
  authenticated surrounding service that does not exist here.
\item
  \textbf{Session-level, not request-level, credential binding.} A
  production design should bind each request to mandate, operation,
  payload digest, audience, expiry, and a replay guard.
\item
  \textbf{Evidence source labels are not authenticated.} See 7.5.
\item
  \textbf{No atomic revocation ordering with a provider.} See 7.4.
\item
  \textbf{Single category, simulated providers, no live result.} The
  natural-language-to-typed-constraint extraction described in 7.3 is
  scripted in the demonstration and not implemented as a model call;
  constraints are authored directly in fixtures.
\item
  \textbf{Canonicalization is custom.} No conformance to a published
  canonicalization scheme {[}15{]} is claimed.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{9.4 Local verification
record}\label{local-verification-record}

On September 19, 2026, against commit \texttt{5c07c79} plus the
demonstration files, \texttt{npm\ run\ build},
\texttt{npx\ tsc\ -\/-noEmit}, \texttt{npm\ test}, and
\texttt{npm\ run\ demo:deterministic} each exited 0, with 253 tests
passing and 8 skipped across 22 files and one skipped file. The identity
suite contained 26 tests, the purchase-mission suite 50, the
demonstration suite 1; the skipped file is a PostgreSQL store suite
requiring a database.

Those totals describe a test suite, not independent validations of this
paper. Passing fixtures against simulated providers do not establish
security, payment correctness, evidence authenticity, or fulfillment
reliability. No financial operation was initiated.

\subsection{10. Evaluation required}\label{evaluation-required}

The central hypothesis, that binding authority to an exact approved
record and a declared configuration prevents failures that amount-based
controls do not, is untested outside local fixtures. It would be
weakened if exact approval provides little protection beyond a
well-designed mandate system, or if its approval burden outweighs the
protection.

\textbf{Comparative study.} The same task set under three
configurations: amount-based limits alone; limits plus exact-record
binding; and both plus a measured deployment. Report blocked
unauthorized operations \emph{and} legitimate operations incorrectly
blocked. A control that stops every bad purchase by stopping most good
ones is not useful.

\textbf{Authority tests.} Undeclared configuration changes, mutable
aliases, stolen and expired credentials, fabricated caller identities,
operator changes, version rollback, missing scopes, and revocation
arriving during capability issuance and during dispatch.

\textbf{Failure injection.} Interrupt each persistence and network
boundary; reorder and duplicate provider events; reuse idempotency keys
past provider retention; simulate partial capture, provider
inconsistency, and refund failure. Report unresolved exposure and time
to reconcile rather than assuming timeouts are safely retryable.

\textbf{Evidence tests.} Fabricated merchant labels, compromised
adapters, conflicting confirmations, cancelled bookings, stale refund
events. Report false success, false failure, and unresolved outcomes
against independently established ground truth.

\textbf{Approval-burden study.} Measure whether people understand what
they authorized, why a change prompt appeared, and the difference
between a refund requested and money returned. Report approval counts,
completion time, abandonment, and misunderstanding alongside safety
outcomes. Given the Pass\^{}10 evidence in Section 3, the interesting
comparison is how burden scales with the number of purchases in a
session.

\textbf{Extraction calibration.} For the PROPOSE phase, measure silent
mistyping (a constraint typed wrongly and enforced) against correct
refusal (a constraint left unresolved and blocking), per Section 7.3.
This is the one evaluation the demonstration cannot supply because its
PROPOSE phase is scripted.

\subsection{11. Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

The public record of agent purchase failures is short, and the reasons
are structural rather than reassuring. The measured record is more
informative: the strongest computer-use agent measured succeeds at least
once on 78\% of tasks and every time on 36\%, and the retail error
category that benchmarks over-represent is the handling of confirmation
itself.

Against that, the controls currently shipped bound the amount of a
transaction. Every failure this survey located was correctly priced. An
issuer's indemnity for agent error is a real backstop and is conditioned
on exactly the artifact this paper proposes to produce. The proposal is
to bind the authorization to an exact record a human approved, evaluate
it with pure functions, fail closed on anything that cannot be
represented, bind authority to a declared software configuration, assess
outcomes from evidence the agent did not author, and treat recovery as a
workflow that reports what it could not recover. No leg of that is new.
The conjunction is, as far as this review could establish, unoccupied.

A prototype implements it for one category against simulated providers,
a demonstration shows the wrong-date candidate refused before the rail
is called, and a read-only inspection of one production rail shows where
the layer would attach and that the rail's own event stream learns about
delivery only as a chargeback reason code. None of that establishes that
the declared software ran, that evidence is authentic, or that any of
this is safe in production. The next contribution should be an
authenticated integration against a real rail, and the negative results
it produces.
